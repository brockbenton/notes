# Peter Singer

## The why and how of effective altruism

*Altruism.* Concern for the well-being and life of others independently of personal benefit. 

Singer presents a though experiment: Say a little girl is ran over by a van in a street. If you are walking by, would you help? Most people would say yes - but Singer asks the following question: Does it really matter that you don't have to walk around these children as you walk down your street? Millions of children die per year, from known-to-be preventable diseases. Can we reduce that death toll?

Much of us spend money on things we don't *need*. Instead, we could give up our money to various charities that are aimed at reducing the exact death toll discussed in the thought experiment.

Billionaires like Bill Gates are able to give a large share of their income and *actually* make an impact - but what if you don't have that type of money? Can you even make an impact with so little money? Singer says there are 4 questions to be answered:

1. *How much of a difference can I make?* A small amount each year adds up. 
2. *Am I expected to abandon my career?* Choose a high-impact career in which you are making a lot of money, so you can give a lot of money. 
3. *Isn't charity bureaucratic and ineffective?* Providing a guide dog for a blind American costs the same as the cost in curing upwards of 2,000 individuals in developing countries. You can research about which organizations are effective are which aren't. 
4. *Isn't it a burden to give up so much?* Many people enjoy giving and receive lots of fulfillment. 

*My Opposition: Everything is self-serving in the technical sense. But even with that, something can be self-serving, and also societally-serving.* 

## What is utilitarianism?

Utilitarianism is an ethical theory that falls under the overarching ethics of consequentualism. The aim of utilitarianism is to maximize utility, or the happiness and well-being of all affected individuals. As such, for utilitarianism, an act is morally good if it produces the greatest happiness or pleasure to the greatest number of people. 

* *Act Utilitarianism.* Judges each action in isolation to determine if it produces the greatest net happiness. 
* *Rule Utilitarianism.* Judges actions based on adherence to moral rules whose general acceptance would produce the best overall consequences for society.

## Discussion #7: Do you think it is possible to forge more broadly effective altruism in our society today?

If we are defining an altruistic act as one that is inherently selfless and aimed at the well-being and betterment of another, then I would claim that it is not possible to forge more broadly effective altruism because altruism doesn't actually exist, at least in the strict sense. The reasoning behind this claim is entirely understood as a consequence of the irreducible axiom that *human action is purposeful* or in other words, all isolated actions are aimed at a particular end - and this end is chosen via a ranking of preferences that are self-determined and thus, self-serving. Using Bill Gates as an example, he is not merely donating to charities for the total benefit of another, but (plausibly) he is donating because he would have otherwise been unfulfilled and dissatisfied with himself if he had not donated. With this in mind, I believe that all action is self-serving in the technical sense, but that there certainly exists acts that are both self-serving and also societally-serving (like the action of donating to a charity that is effectively allocating its donors funds). 

If we are instead broadly defining altruistic acts as something that might be technically self-serving, but also societally-serving or community-serving or another-person-other-than-myself-serving, then there are certainly ways to forge more broadly effective "altruism" in our society today, particularly through the education and implementation of market-based solutions. The advantage of a typical charity is that it can often solve an immediate solution relatively quickly, but its disadvantage is that these often quick solutions are band-aids on top of much deeper wounds. Charities are often focused on very particular issues - rightfully so as they are cheaper and easier to fix - that are merely a symptom of a more systemic issue. Toby Ord, the founder of *Giving What We Can* asks people to pledge 10 percent of their lifetime earnings to fighting global poverty. He calculated that some charities are hundreds or even thousands of times more effective than others. If effectiveness varies that much, the question of which interventions address root causes matters enormously. For example, in many countries within Africa and southern Asia, starting a profitable and effective business is nearly impossible due to the lack of property rights, dysfunctional market structures, and the sheer amount of (corrupt) bureaucracy that must be endured. Charities can fill some of the gaps in these countries, but will significantly struggle at making permanent change.

Market structures that promote the necessary conditions for flourishing competition allow for solutions that are beyond immediate remedy. This competition is long lasting, and if solutions aren't sufficient then it is assumed that another firm will step in to fill the gap. If locations are too poor to pay for any sort of goods or services a firm provides, even if the consumers are in great need of it, this typically entails a lack of institutions which allow poor people to earn enough to become customers. The only reason Matt Wage is able to donate so much money to charity is because the institutions in America are strong and effective. Therefore, the long-term fix is building those instutions, and charity can only buy time until then.
