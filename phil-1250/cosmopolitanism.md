# Cosmopolitanism

*According to Nussbaum, what is Cosmopolitanism? In discussing the early antecedents of Cosmopolitanism, Nussbaum discusses the views of Cicero (Roman statesman and philosopher, 50's BC) and Marcus Aurelius (the Roman Stoic emperor, 2nd century AD), particularly with respect the idea of equal human dignity. In relation to this discussion, what is the "bifurcation of duties" Nussbaum critiques? Finally, do you find the idea of "world-citizen" as a possible description of yourself compelling? Explain.*

Cosmopolitanism, as defined by Nussbaum, is a moral and political ideal that every person is fundamentally a citizen of a single global community, rather than being defined by traits such as nation, social class, status, or gender. She states that the core of this ideal is the recognition of the non-hierarchical, and thus equal, worth of all human beings. A being that possesses any sort of capacity for agency is valued the same as any other beings that possess any sort of capacity for agency. People are prescribed traits by accident, like birth and national origin, that shouldn't have political implications.

The "Bifurcation of Duties" is the term coined for the fatal flaw in classical Stoic and Ciceronian thought: the splitting of moral obligion into two categories, namely Duties of Justice and Duties of Material Aid. The Duties of Justice employs strict obligition to respect others, refrain from aggressive war, support victims of unjustice, and forbid any sort of crimes against humanity. The Duties of Material Aid state obligations to provide material welfare to people in need. Cicero and the rest of the Stoics argued that diginity is a choice that is completely immune to the accidents of fortune - money, rank, and physical possessions are unnecessary for a flourishing moral life. Failing to provide material aid doesn't erode a person's core dignity. Thus, that nations and individuals owe others strict non-aggression, but no material aid.

Nussbaum critiques this bifurcation on three grounds: (1) It ignores material inequalities that are primarily caused by accidental birth in one country or another. (2) It relies on a false distinction regarding cost in which the tradtiion pretends that fulfilling duties of justice is expenditure-free while material aid is costly. This is not necessarily true as the defense of our allies often requires massive financial expenditure, potentially exceeding what it would cost to end world hunger. (3) It is philosophically incoherent because if human dignity was truly immune, then slavery and torture would not harm a person's dignity any more than hunger or disease. First-generation rights (political liberty and justice) necessarily require second-generation rights (economic and social support). 

The ideal "world-citizen" for Nassbaum is attractive on some fronts and less than ideal on other fronts. The idea that humans are inherently equal is a completely logical and defensible belief - and one that is incredibly pragmatic for any sort of moral society. The extension of this is that we must develop systems that protect this idea - not monolithic world States that edge on tyranny. Nonetheless, I find that Nassbaum's duty of material aid not as attractive. Any sort of positive right I am opposed for on the basis that I am not a fan of force, regardless of if it will result in betterment or not. Positive rights are comparable to compulsion and slavery to me - both of which I am certainly against.
